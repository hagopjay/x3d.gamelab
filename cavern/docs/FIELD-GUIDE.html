01 / FIRST DESCENT

A line through the mountain.

Begin with the default assisted flight and a chase camera. The course descends through a broad natural passage into a constructed light world, then returns to rock. Fifteen gates give the eye a destination over a 2,400 m course while the cavern walls make velocity tangible. The final “Into the light” chapter is a visual finish; this prototype does not continue into an exterior landing or parachute simulation. The orange wing membranes, dark harness and bright helmet make the pilot’s pose readable against both environments.

Make small bank inputs, release them, and watch the turn develop. Pitch changes the aerodynamic state; it does not directly move the pilot up or down. Try a short flare before a gate, then allow the suit to regain speed. Go limp in a spacious chamber to see how the articulated body responds, and recover before asking for a precise line.

The on-screen controls are authoritative for the current build. The route coach applies limited bank and angle-of-attack guidance without moving the pilot directly. Manual input takes priority independently on each axis; with only bank input, for example, the coach can still guide pitch. Switch it off to control both axes directly. The harness is wearable equipment around the body, not a tether attached to the cavern.

The challenge is composure, not maximum speed. A fast approach leaves less time to correct. A clean gate and a controlled recovery tell you more about the flight state than frantic alternating inputs.

02 / READ THE INSTRUMENTS

Two speeds. One body.

Airspeed measures motion relative to the surrounding air. Groundspeed here is horizontal world speed, √(vₓ² + v_z²), relative to the cavern. Lift and drag use airspeed; your arrival at the next gate depends on world motion. A tailwind can make the walls rush past without producing the same change in aerodynamic load.

Use the gate sequence as a route and the instruments as an explanation. Near-wall flight is exciting, but a near-miss score is a game rule rather than a safety metric. A rock ornament can be presentation detail; the documented collision envelope owns physical contact.

03 / WHAT MOVES THE PILOT

Gravity powers the descent.

The flight model works in metres, seconds and kilograms. It computes the pilot’s velocity relative to a prescribed wind field and uses dynamic pressure to evaluate aerodynamic forces. This follows the standard lift and drag framework described by NASA [1, 2]. The suit coefficients are deliberately exposed and remain uncalibrated model choices.

u = v − w(x,t)
q = ½ ρ |u|²
L = q S CL   D = q S CD
a = (FL + FD)/m + g
v: world velocity · w: local wind velocity · ρ: air density · S: reference suit area. Drag opposes u; lift acts perpendicular to u.

Bank tilts the lift direction. Pitch changes the modeled aerodynamic coefficients, including a smooth stall reduction at sufficiently high angle of attack. The drag polar includes a base term and a term proportional to lift coefficient squared, following the structure of an induced-drag model [3]. Its numerical constants are authored for this prototype; they are not fitted to a particular pilot or suit.

DEFAULT MODEL

A transparent starting point

Mass 90 kg; area 1.6 m²; density 1.225 kg/m³; gravity 9.80665 m/s². Nominal trim CL = 0.55; CD = 0.14 + 0.24 CL², before the additional state-dependent controls.

ANALYTIC CHECK

A scale for intuition

With those constant trim coefficients, ideal still-air steady glide gives L/D ≈ 2.59, speed ≈ 39 m/s and sink ≈ 14 m/s. This is an equilibrium calculation for the simplified force law, not a measured flight result.

The pilot has 14 mass-weighted particles and 24 distance/bracing constraints, solved with five XPBD iterations per substep. One whole-body aerodynamic resultant is distributed by particle mass. The body’s centre of mass comes from those dynamic particles; it is not a kinematic root dragging a cosmetic puppet. Active pose forces have their mass-weighted mean removed, so they supply no net linear thrust. This does not establish exact angular-momentum conservation or a resolved pressure distribution over each wing.

The harness, limbs and wing surfaces make forces legible without paying for high-resolution fabric simulation. Limp mode disables active pose restoration and reduces modeled lift while increasing drag. XPBD expresses constraint softness with a compliance term scaled by the timestep [4]. Anatomical joint stops, per-membrane aerodynamics, fabric elasticity and full rigid-body inertia remain outside this approximation.

Accuracy has layers. Correct force directions and units are necessary. Real suit coefficients, unsteady aerodynamics, fabric deformation, body inertia and measured validation are additional work. This prototype is for play and investigation, not real-world flight planning.

The wind is a smooth prescribed field with adjustable mean speed, direction and gust strength. The demo does not solve pressure-driven cavern airflow, turbulence around stalactites, wake interaction or compressible flow. A beautiful stream of dust illustrates the environment; it does not establish a CFD result.

04 / SIX INVESTIGATIONS

Change one thing. Keep the question.

Pause before editing, reset for each comparison, and retain the same camera and control strategy. Assistance actively changes inputs, so turn it off when comparing the uncontrolled physical response. An early collision can terminate a run before the aerodynamic behavior you wanted to observe.

Follow the air, then the stone

Try: Compare zero wind with equal-strength headwind and tailwind. Keep mass, density, suit coefficients and gusts fixed.

Notice: Gate arrival time and airspeed need not change together. At the same world velocity, changing wind changes relative airspeed and therefore the forces; at a settled aerodynamic state, wind changes world travel.

Give a crosswind time to speak

Try: Add a modest sideways wind, first with assistance and then without it. Release steering briefly in a broad chamber.

Notice: The assistance system compensates through control inputs. Direct flight reveals drift. Neither outcome means the wind is absent; the controller and the disturbance both contribute to the observed line.

Double speed, quadruple the question

Try: Compare two moments with similar pose and substantially different airspeed. Read q and aerodynamic forces together.

Notice: Dynamic pressure grows with speed squared. Doubling speed gives four times q at fixed density. Forces only follow the same ratio if coefficients and area remain unchanged; a flare or stall breaks that comparison.

Carry more, or fly thinner air

Try: Increase mass, then separately reduce density. Restart each experiment instead of changing both at once.

Notice: Gravity acceleration is independent of mass. At a fixed velocity the same aerodynamic force accelerates a heavier body less. For unchanged trim coefficients, equilibrium speed scales approximately with √(m/ρS); wing loading connects several sliders through one relationship.

Spend speed in a flare

Try: Compare a short flare with a long held flare on the same straight. Watch sink, speed and drag rather than only the pilot’s pitch.

Notice: A transient can improve the immediate path while consuming kinetic energy. Holding a strong input can accumulate drag or reach the heuristic stall region. A flare is not an engine, and the lift peak is not an unlimited climb command.

Find the useful softness

Try: Compare a firmer and softer ragdoll in the same gentle gust field, then briefly release the pose.

Notice: Compliance changes joint response and silhouette. Too much softness reduces pilot readability; too much stiffness makes a small solver’s errors more visible. Treat the setting as an effective model parameter rather than a direct measurement of fabric elasticity or anatomy.

05 / SPEND THE FRAME BUDGET

Keep the equations small.
Make the light do the work.

A small fixed-step model separates gameplay physics from display refresh. The application advances 120 Hz ticks, with adaptive subdivisions targeting at most 0.2 m of particle travel inside each tick. Swept joint contacts sample the travelled segment and bisect the first wall-field crossing. Five constraint iterations follow, with additional envelope corrections. The clock runs at most eight ticks per display frame so a stalled browser does not enter an ever-growing simulation backlog. When that budget is exceeded, simulation time can fall behind wall time; diagnostics should make this visible.

The visual strategy concentrates detail in the visible tunnel: faceted rock, repeated instanced accents, emissive portals, dust and atmospheric depth. Bloom creates a strong luminous response without making every accent a shadow-casting light. The pilot’s wing membranes follow the compact rig rather than thousands of simulated cloth vertices.

IF THE FRAME RATE DROPS

Reduce display cost first

Choose a lower graphics preset and turn off expensive display effects. Keep physics settings unchanged while comparing. Resolution, bloom, geometry and draw calls affect different parts of the frame.

WHAT A COUNTER CAN PROVE

Measure the right boundary

CPU simulation time describes the solver on this machine. Render submission time and draw calls describe display work. None alone establishes whole-frame GPU time or equivalent performance on another PC.

Fast contact needs a separate budget: collision checks must account for motion between states, and constraint corrections must remain compatible with the cavern envelope. A small timestep helps but does not, by itself, prove that all high-speed contacts are detected. The validation record distinguishes tested cases from wider guarantees.

06 / WHAT X3D CARRIES

A scene you can inspect.

GameLab’s strongest transferable principle is that the model should remain legible outside its renderer. CHASM exports an X3D 4.0 presentation snapshot so the cavern and pilot can be inspected with standard scene tools. The snapshot contains the cave, gates, pilot articulation and membranes. A disabled RigidBodyCollection records 14 particle proxies, masses and velocities; custom metadata records distance-constraint and settings data. Those inspectable records expose what was present at export time [5, 6]. Decorative light and dust are omitted.

BoundaryMeaning
Standard scene geometryPortable shapes, coordinates, materials and transforms describe the exported view.
Application metadataThe chasm-x3d-snapshot/1 contract records settings and custom XPBD distance constraints.
Custom flight semanticsThe JavaScript aerodynamic law, assistance, scoring and wind field belong to this application.
Ragdoll behaviorThe disabled particle-body graph is a record. Its custom constraints are not executable standard X3D joint semantics.
Snapshot vs checkpointA static scene export is not a resumable simulation or an exact deterministic replay.

X3D’s RigidBodyPhysics component describes bodies and joints, but standard node names do not make two implementations numerically equivalent. CHASM’s export contract should state which semantics are standard, which are metadata, and which remain custom. A schema pass establishes a particular structural check; it does not validate the suit’s aerodynamics or the pilot’s safety. CHASM does not import arbitrary X3D scenes. It imports its own settings JSON after validating the entire candidate, rejecting unknown fields, nonfinite numbers, unsupported choices and files larger than 8 KiB before changing the experiment.

CSV capture samples telemetry roughly ten times per simulation second and keeps at most 20,000 rows. Each row includes the live physical parameters, so sampled wind and tuning changes remain interpretable. The header records export-time presentation/settings; input history and internal solver state are absent. Save separate settings files and restart between controlled comparisons; neither CSV nor settings JSON is a resumable checkpoint.

Preserve the difference between evidence and illustration. The lit rock is the visual scene. The analytic envelope is the collision model. The source files and validation record explain their relationship.

07 / LESSONS TO CARRY UPSTREAM

The next useful descent.

Share boundaries before sharing code. A stable interface between course geometry, collision, flight state and rendering prevents a visual improvement from quietly changing the physical model. Give the environment and every gate a persistent identity; score an actual crossing once rather than rewarding proximity every frame.

Keep assistance observable. Its active corrections are part of the explanation. A future flight recorder should store control inputs, parameter changes, wind identity and simulation timing alongside poses. A pose trace alone cannot reproduce a controlled run.

Validate a hierarchy. Start with vector directions, units, symmetry and a still-air equilibrium. Add fixed-step scheduling, gust response, extreme settings and swept contacts. Only then compare against measured suit data. Improvements to the renderer should have their own screenshots and performance observations, separate from physical acceptance tests.

Useful next research includes experimentally informed coefficient tables, improved anatomical joint limits, distributed aerodynamic loading on individual membranes, and a collision mesh derived directly from the displayed cavern. Each extension should answer a specific question before increasing runtime cost.
